WIMS — June 2026 Fasting Report Presentation

Introduction

1.  This presentation is about a personal experiment with fasting and
    weight measurement.
2.  The experiment ran from September 2025 to January 2026.
3.  Rather than presenting fasting as a solution, the purpose was to
    observe what happened.
4.  The interesting part turned out to be the measurement and feedback
    process.
5.  The presentation therefore moves from what happened, to what it
    might mean, and finally to what questions remain.

[SLIDE 1] — June Fasting Report 2026

1.  This presentation summarises the June 2026 Fasting Report.
2.  It covers a defined period from September 2025 to January 2026.
3.  The study records personal experience with fasting and weight
    measurement.
4.  The emphasis is on observation, measurement and reflection.
5.  The wider idea that emerged is weight management as a feedback
    system.

[SLIDE 2] — June 2026 Fasting Report

1.  The full title is Weight Management as Measurement and a Feedback
    System.
2.  It is a reflective, data-led personal case study.
3.  The observation period was September 2025 to January 2026.
4.  The report is based on personal data rather than a formal scientific
    study.
5.  The purpose is to understand what happened and what was learnt.

[SLIDE 3] — Health Warning

1.  This is a personal case study.
2.  It is not medical advice.
3.  It is not a formal scientific study.
4.  It does not provide a recommendation for others to follow.
5.  The presentation describes what happened and what was learnt.

[SLIDE 4] — Why Was This Study Undertaken?

1.  The starting point was observation and curiosity.
2.  Weight was recorded consistently.
3.  The aim was to identify patterns rather than react to individual
    readings.
4.  Measurements were used to support reflection.
5.  The study asks what the measurements might tell us about managing
    weight.

[SLIDE 5] — Background

1.  Weight management is not simply a nutritional problem.
2.  Food quality and quantity can influence weight.
3.  Exercise and activity also matter.
4.  Quality of life and personal reasons are important.
5.  Sustainable change tends to come from small, repeatable actions
    rather than willpower alone.

[SLIDE 6] — Method & Constraints

1.  Weight was recorded daily in a spreadsheet.
2.  Weekly averages were calculated.
3.  Weekly results were compared with targets.
4.  Morning/rested weight was used as the first measurement.
5.  Weekly trends were considered more useful than individual
    fluctuations.

[SLIDE 7] — Reflection

1.  The report uses three questions: What? So What? What Next?
2.  What? describes what happened and what was measured.
3.  So What? asks what the observations mean.
4.  What Next? considers future action and changes.
5.  This framework turns measurement into learning rather than simply
    recording numbers.

[SLIDE 8] — September 2025

1.  September began with an average weight of 16.51 stone.
2.  Weight fell to 15.59 stone by Week 4.
3.  Week 5 showed a rebound to 16.04 stone.
4.  Fasting varied between two and four days per week.
5.  Despite the fluctuation, the overall monthly trend was downward.

[SLIDE 9] — November 2025

1.  November continued the downward trend.
2.  Average weight moved from 15.25 to 14.78 stone.
3.  Fasting increased progressively from one to four days per week.
4.  The rate of reduction was slower than September and October.
5.  Increasing fasting frequency did not produce the same acceleration
    seen earlier.

NOTE: The current presentation’s visible November table contains
September-style figures. The main report is the source of truth for the
November figures.

[SLIDE 10] — December 2025

1.  December was deliberately different from the preceding months.
2.  Weight measurement and fasting were paused over Christmas.
3.  No weekly data is presented for December.
4.  This creates a natural interruption in the observation period.
5.  It provides a useful contrast with periods of active measurement and
    fasting.

[SLIDE 11] — January 2026

1.  Measurement and fasting resumed in January.
2.  Average weight began at approximately 15.26 stone.
3.  Three fasting days per week accompanied gradual reduction during the
    first three weeks.
4.  The final week had no fasting and average weight increased to 15.50
    stone.
5.  Overall, weight appeared to stabilise around approximately 15 stone.

[SLIDE 12] — The Full Data Set

1.  This slide brings the weekly measurements together.
2.  Daily measurements were used to calculate weekly averages.
3.  The weekly average provides a clearer view of the trend.
4.  Weekly change is a separate measurement.
5.  The two measurements answer different questions about what happened.

[SLIDE 13] — September–November 2025

1.  The period shows a substantial overall reduction.
2.  The starting average was 16.51 stone.
3.  The trend reached approximately 14.78 stone by late November.
4.  Average weight and start-to-end weekly change are related but
    different measures.
5.  The distinction becomes important when interpreting short-term
    fluctuations.

[SLIDE 14] — The Overall Picture

1.  This is the visual summary of the September-to-January period.
2.  The overall direction is downward.
3.  There are normal fluctuations within that overall trend.
4.  December represents the deliberate break in measurement.
5.  January demonstrates what happened when measurement and fasting
    resumed.

[SLIDE 15] — Analysis & Reflection

1.  The key observation was that fasting alone did not tell the whole
    story.
2.  Measurement provided continuous feedback.
3.  Weekly trends were more useful than individual daily readings.
4.  Fasting and gentle activity appeared to work as complementary tools.
5.  The combination of measurement, feedback and adjustment became the
    more important idea.

[SLIDE 16] — Measurement as a Feedback System

1.  Daily weight provides the basic measurement.
2.  Weekly averages reveal the trend.
3.  The trend provides feedback.
4.  Behaviour can then be adjusted.
5.  The numbers become a signal rather than a series of individual
    successes or failures.

[SLIDE 17] — From Dieting to Adjustment

1.  Traditional dieting can encourage an all-or-nothing mindset.
2.  The report compares this with a light switch.
3.  A feedback approach is more like a dimmer switch.
4.  Small adjustments can be made as weight changes.
5.  The aim is not perfection but gradual adjustment over time.

[SLIDE 18] — The Role of Activity During Fasting

1.  Activity during the study was generally gentle and sustained.
2.  Walking, housework and hobbies were typical examples.
3.  The focus was roughly one or two hours rather than intensive
    exercise.
4.  Earlier observations suggested activity before breaking a fast
    appeared noticeable.
5.  Activity was considered alongside fasting rather than as a separate
    solution.

[SLIDE 19] — Why Might Fasting Work?

1.  There is a behavioural explanation as well as a biological one.
2.  Fasting means fewer meals.
3.  It also means fewer food-related decisions.
4.  The report discusses changes in blood sugar, glycogen and use of
    stored fat.
5.  The important observation is that behaviour and biology appear to
    work together.

[SLIDE 20] — Why Fasting Is Considered to Work

1.  A practical explanation may be more important than a complex
    theoretical one.
2.  Fasting reduced overall food intake in this case.
3.  It simplified food-related decision-making.
4.  This made the fasting pattern easier to sustain.
5.  The report remains cautious about claiming a definitive biological
    explanation.

[SLIDE 21] — Fridge and Freezer Metaphor

1.  The metaphor provides a simple way of visualising energy stores.
2.  Glycogen is represented as the easily accessible fridge.
3.  Body fat is represented as the longer-term freezer.
4.  Fasting is presented as allowing the easier-access store to be used
    first.
5.  The metaphor is a teaching device rather than a complete biological
    model.

[SLIDE 22] — Fasting Is a Method, But Not the Whole Story

1.  Fasting proved useful in the case study.
2.  But fasting was only one part of the process.
3.  Measurement provided feedback.
4.  Feedback allowed adjustments to behaviour.
5.  The bigger idea is measurement + feedback + willingness to adjust.

[SLIDE 23] — What Was Learnt?

1.  Regular measurement matters.
2.  Weekly trends matter more than individual daily fluctuations.
3.  Short repeated fasts appeared easier to sustain than prolonged
    restriction.
4.  Weight management can be viewed as a feedback-control problem.
5.  The learning came from observing the process over time.

[SLIDE 24] — What Is Significant?

1.  Weight reduced from approximately 16.5 stone towards approximately
    15 stone.
2.  This occurred without calorie counting.
3.  Weight increased when measurement and fasting stopped.
4.  Measurement appeared to influence behaviour.
5.  These observations generated further questions rather than a final
    answer.

[SLIDE 25] — Questions Raised

1.  The report deliberately finishes by identifying questions for
    further investigation.
2.  These include blood sugar and low blood sugar.
3.  It asks about glycogen depletion and weight fluctuations.
4.  It considers whether measurements could help forecast potential
    weight loss.
5.  It also raises the question of how someone inexperienced with
    fasting might approach it — a question for future work, not an
    instruction from this report.

[SLIDE 26] — Note on Measurement

1.  Measurement was not perfect.
2.  Sometimes the best weight during a day was recorded rather than a
    fixed-time measurement.
3.  Spreadsheet data entry introduces the possibility of human error.
4.  A simple measurement and control system could potentially support
    behavioural adjustment.
5.  Measurement nevertheless remained critical to the process.

[SLIDE 27] — A Defined Period of Observation

1.  The report is deliberately presented as a defined period of
    observation.
2.  It does not claim to provide a finished answer.
3.  Meaningful weight change occurred during the observed period.
4.  Measurement created greater awareness of food choices.
5.  The key lesson was managing weight over time realistically and
    sustainably.

[SLIDE 28] — Questions for More Structured Research

1.  The case study has generated further research questions.
2.  One concerns a defended or stabilised weight range.
3.  Another concerns fasting duration, glycogen depletion and ketones.
4.  Low-intensity activity while fasted warrants further investigation.
5.  The effect of regular measurement and feedback on behaviour is
    another important question.

[TAIL]

Closing Conclusion

1.  This was a defined period of observation, not a finished scientific
    answer.
2.  Fasting appeared useful, but it was not the whole story.
3.  Measurement and feedback changed the way the weight data was
    understood.
4.  The process became one of observing, learning and making small
    adjustments.
5.  The most important lesson was not how to lose weight quickly, but
    how to manage weight over time in a realistic and sustainable way.

Final thought:

Weight management can be viewed not as a one-time problem solved by the
perfect diet, but as an ongoing process of measurement, observation,
feedback and adjustment.
